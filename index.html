Mobile app, 2026

8=1 UABC

A student app for UABC's 8=1 program, where every eight cultural, sports, academic or health activities earn one elective credit. I designed the mobile app in Figma and built it in Flutter.

Role
Mobile UX/UI design, full Flutter app and API integration
Team
5 students, I own the mobile app
Tools
Figma, Flutter, Dart

A student needs three things: find an activity worth going to, prove they were there, and know how close they are to the next credit. Usually while standing at the door of an event.

Design decisions

  • Progress comes first

    "How many do I have left?" is the question students open the app with, so the home screen answers it before they tap anything: eight check circles, then the same thing in plain words. Check-in sits right below as one full-width card, because it's the action students take at every event.

  • A way in when the camera fails

    Scanning a QR code is the fastest way to check in, but virtual events have no code to scan, and cameras, bad lighting and cracked screens can all fail. The same screen offers a manual code, so a student can always check in.

  • Filters that tell you the result

    Filters open in a bottom sheet, so the calendar stays visible behind it. The button says "Show 3 activities" instead of "Apply", so students never land on an empty list by surprise.

  • Less to type, fewer typos

    The @uabc.edu.mx part of the email is filled in, so students type only their username. Sign-up is two short steps with a progress bar.

  • Clear about what the app can't fix

    Profile data comes from the university's records, and the app can't change them. So there's no fake "Edit" button. The profile tells students exactly who to email if something is wrong.

Never color alone

Each category always shows its label next to its color, so the meaning doesn't depend on seeing color.

Design that survives code

When I built the screens in Flutter, I put the colors and shared widgets in one theme file, so the app matches the Figma file screen for screen.

Desktop app, 2025

Laminar

A desktop app that lets engineering students version their projects without the command line: open a folder, see what changed, sync to GitHub. I designed it alone, from first wireframe to working app.

Role

Team

Tools

Web redesign and usability test, 2025

Catálogo Cimarrón

A redesign of UABC's library catalog. With a teammate, I mapped how students look for a book, prototyped a new catalog, and tested it with students. Every task succeeded, and the test still found a critical problem.

Role
UX research and UI design, in a pair
Course
Human-Computer Interaction
Methods
HTA, storyboards, think-aloud test, SUS

  1. Task analysis

    Broke "find a book" into 10 subgoals, from search to shelf location.

  2. Storyboards

    Sketched the main flows with what the student feels at each step.

  3. Prototype

    12 Figma screens: search, results, record, lists, loans, profile.

  4. Usability test

    Moderated think-aloud sessions with 6 timed tasks, then a SUS survey.

100% task success across 6 tasks with 2 students.

88.75 average SUS score, against a benchmark of about 68.

42 s to find "purchase suggestions", the slowest task.

“It says available, but how do I get it? Do I have to go to the library?”

Test participant, psychology student

What the numbers didn't show

  • Critical

    No way to reserve a book. Both students looked for a "Reserve" button on an available book. One clicked "Save record" hoping it would work.

  • Medium

    Purchase suggestions were hidden in the side menu. Neither student saw them at first.

  • Medium

    "Available" didn't say how many. One student asked: "So they only have one?"

  • Low

    Leftover placeholder text on the welcome screen confused both students.

What I'd change next

Make "Place hold" the main action on any available book. Show the exact count, like "2 of 3 copies at Biblioteca Central". Put purchase suggestions on the empty search results page, where the need actually comes up.

What I learned

A high success rate can hide a critical problem, and listening to students mattered more than the scores. Looking back, the heavy glass style feels busy to me. My later work, like 8=1, shows where my visual taste has gone since.
